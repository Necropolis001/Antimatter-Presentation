What is antimatter, and could it really power our future?

What Is Antimatter?

Everything around us is made of matter: tiny particles like electrons and protons. Antimatter is made of matching antiparticles, which have the same mass but the opposite electric charge.

Protons have antiprotons too. Paul Dirac predicted antimatter in 1928, and the positron was discovered in 1932. Antimatter is not the same thing as dark matter.

If antimatter is real, what kind of energy source would it be?

What Type of Energy Source Is It?

Renewable

Naturally replenished. The sun keeps shining and the wind keeps blowing.

Solar, wind, water, geothermal

Nonrenewable

Mined or pumped out of the earth. Once it's used up, it's gone.

Coal, oil, natural gas, uranium

Antimatter: neither one

We can't mine it or collect it from nature. It has to be made, and making it takes energy.

Closer to a battery or hydrogen fuel

That makes antimatter an energy carrier, a way to store energy rather than a source of it. It can be made over and over, but making it takes enormous amounts of energy, more than it ever gives back.

If we have to make it, where does it come from?

Where Does Antimatter Come From?

Antimatter doesn't occur naturally, except for single particles made by nuclear or cosmic ray interactions.

Cosmic rays crash into our atmosphere and create antiparticles. In 2011, a satellite found antiprotons trapped in a belt around Earth.

NASA's Fermi telescope caught thunderstorms shooting out beams of positrons, and some radioactive atoms give off positrons too.

So how do we get it?

The only way to get antimatter is to produce it, which mostly happens in a particle accelerator. No facility in North Carolina makes it on a scale that could be used for energy, and you can't buy it in Fayetteville or anywhere else in the U.S.

How do scientists make it on purpose?

How Scientists Make Antimatter

Anti-atoms are only made at CERN's "Antimatter Factory" in Geneva, Switzerland. There, protons going just under the speed of light are smashed into an iridium metal target to make antiprotons.

10 trillion protonshit the target in each shot, about every 2 minutes
~30 million antiprotonscome out the other end, slowed down and ready to trap
5 × 10−17 gramsThat's what all 30 million antiprotons weigh together, so making even 1 gram is out of reach.

What happens when antimatter finally touches matter?

How Does Antimatter Produce Energy?

When antimatter comes into contact with matter, the two particles annihilate. Both disappear and their mass turns into energy.

E = mc2
Eenergy mmass cspeed of light (300,000 km per second)

c² is a huge number, so even a tiny bit of mass equals a lot of energy. In annihilation, all of the mass turns into energy.

Electron + positron make two gamma rays (high-energy light).

Proton + antiproton make pions, which break down into gamma rays, neutrinos and other particles.

In theory, that energy could become heat and then electricity, but nobody has built anything that does this yet.

It sounds like only a giant lab could make antimatter, but your kitchen makes some too.

Tiny amounts aside, what makes antimatter so attractive?

The Three Biggest Advantages

1

Extraordinary energy density

A very small amount of antimatter can release an enormous amount of energy when annihilated with matter. It releases all of its energy, compared to less than 1% for nuclear fusion.

2

No carbon dioxide

The annihilation reaction doesn't burn fossil fuels, so it gives off no carbon dioxide or air pollution.

Making it is a different story (coming up later).

3

Space exploration potential

Antimatter could theoretically power advanced spacecraft. According to NASA, it holds about 10 billion times more energy per unit of mass than regular rocket fuel. It's only on paper so far.

Why aren't we using it already?

The Challenges

1Extreme production costs

$62.5 trillion per gram

Antimatter is the most expensive material in the world. NASA's 1999 estimate of $62.5 trillion per gram is more than half of the whole world economy (about $117 trillion).

It's an estimate, not a real price, because nobody sells antimatter.

2Negative energy return

~1 billion × more energy in than out

It takes more energy to make antimatter than it gives back, so it loses energy overall. You only get back about one billionth of the energy you put in.

3Difficult storage and containment

Antimatter is extremely hard to store. It can't go in an ordinary container, because touching ordinary matter causes annihilation, so it has to float in electromagnetic traps.

CERN has stored antiprotons for over a year. In 2026, it moved 92 of them by truck, in a trap weighing almost a tonne.

4Hard to convert into electricity

Nobody has built a machine that turns annihilation into usable electricity. The high-energy radiation it gives off would also need thick shielding.

Could it ever power anything we actually use?

Could It Actually Power Our World?

Possible today

Medicine: PET scans

Not as a power source, but Fayetteville already uses antimatter in PET scans at Cape Fear Valley Medical Center. A tiny radioactive tracer gives off positrons, and the scanner uses them to find problems like cancer.

Scientific research

Labs like CERN study antimatter to learn why the universe is made of matter at all.

Still theoretical

Not realistic

Homes and schools

No. All the antimatter CERN has ever made could only light a single light bulb for a few minutes. It's far too hard to produce and store, so we'd need better technology and a safe way to store it first.

Maybe someday

Transportation and space travel

Possibly in the future, because it releases a huge amount of energy that could power spacecraft or other vehicles. But producing and storing it is too difficult for our current resources. The first trap to carry antiprotons by truck weighs almost a tonne.

What should the United States do?

Environmental Impact and Presidential Recommendation

The good news

  • No pollution or greenhouse gases when it releases energy
  • The annihilation itself wouldn't make much sound

The catch

  • Making and storing it takes loads of electricity and special equipment
  • The factories and power plants behind it can still cause pollution
  • Accelerators, magnets and cooling systems run constantly, so there would be noise around the facility
  • It can't fix our pollution problems now, because it uses far more energy than it gives back

Madam President: invest in the science, not the power grid.

Don't

Try to replace fossil fuels with antimatter. It costs trillions of dollars per gram, and even if CERN did nothing but make antimatter, it could only make about a billionth of a gram per year.

Do

  1. Put energy money into solar, wind and nuclear power, which already work on a large scale
  2. Keep funding antimatter research: cheaper production, better magnetic traps, particle physics and spacecraft propulsion

Antimatter is already helping people through PET scans that can find cancer, and CERN now traps antihydrogen 8 times faster than before (2025). With advances in technology, it could theoretically become a reliable fuel in the future.

The challenge isn't discovering the energy antimatter contains.
It's learning how to harness it.
